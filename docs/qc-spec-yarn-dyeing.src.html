Read section 2 first. This specification rests on four assumptions about your fibre, process and laboratory capability. If any of them is wrong, the characteristic tables and tolerance limits change materially. Nothing should be built until those four are confirmed.

1. What this document is

Three inspection stage gates for batch-wise package dyeing of filament yarn, specified to international test standards, and the three tablet applications that capture them. It covers what is measured, to which standard, against what limit, on how many samples, how often, and who decides.

It deliberately stops short of code. The characteristic tables in sections 5–7 are the part that needs your judgement as much as mine, and they are cheaper to argue about now than after they are built into master data.

2. Assumptions that must be confirmed

I have inferred the process from your own data model rather than assuming a generic textile plant. Each inference below is stated with its evidence so you can correct it quickly.

AssumptionEvidence in your systemIf wrong
A1 — Synthetic filament yarn, most likely polyester DTY/FDY, not staple or natural fibre ZZMARA carries ZZDENIR (denier) and ZZFILAM (filament count). Denier and filament count are filament-yarn attributes; staple yarn is specified in Ne/Nm with a count and twist. Cotton or blends would replace disperse-dye tests with reactive-dye tests, add mercerisation and singeing checks, and change every fastness limit. Roughly half of sections 5–6 would be rewritten.
A2 — Package (cheese) dyeing in HTHP vessels, not hank or beam ZPP_BATCHN has a CHEESES quantity per batch. A cheese is a dye package. Hank dyeing removes inside–outside package variation as a characteristic and changes the sampling unit from package to hank bundle.
A3 — The plant batch is the quality unit ZPP_BATCHN links GREY_CODE → DYE_CODE with AUFNR, BCHDATE, QTY and CHEESES. Operators already work to this number. If quality is judged per production order or per customer lot instead, the inspection lot anchor changes.
A4 — Winding is a separate downstream step producing the saleable cone Separate packing tables (ZPP_PACK), a Packing Register, and winding referenced in the plant runbook (ZJOBREPTN — dyeing / winding job cards). If winding is inline with dyeing, stages 2 and 3 merge into one gate.
A fifth unknown, and the most important one: what does your laboratory actually have? A specification that calls for xenon-arc light fastness or Classimat fault classification is worthless if the instruments are not on site. Section 14 lists the equipment each characteristic requires. Please mark that list before we go further — I would rather cut a characteristic than specify a test that quietly never gets done.

3. Process model and where the gates sit

Three gates, each with a different question to answer and a different cost of being wrong.

GateMaterial stateQuestion it answersCost of a missSAP anchor
Stage 1
Raw material		Greige / RFD yarn, undyed packagesWill this yarn dye level, and will it survive the dyebath?Highest. A dye-affinity fault is invisible until after dyeing, and by then the batch is scrap or a re-dye.ZPP_BATCHN.GREY_CODE, inspection type 01 at goods receipt
Stage 2
After dyeing		Dyed, dried packagesIs the shade right, is it level, is it fast, and is the yarn undamaged?High. Detected here it is a re-dye; missed here it becomes a customer claim.ZPP_BATCHN.DYE_CODE + AUFNR, inspection type 03 in-process
Stage 3
After winding		Wound cones, ready to packIs the package sound, and did winding damage the yarn or the shade?Moderate but frequent. Winding faults are the commonest cause of running complaints at the customer's machine.ZPP_PACK / HU, inspection type 04 at GR from production

Why stage 1 carries the most weight

This is the point most plants under-invest in, and it is where I would put the effort first. Dye uniformity in polyester is governed by properties fixed long before the yarn reaches you — draw ratio, heater profile, spin finish level, crystallinity. A package that differs in dye affinity from its neighbours produces barré that no amount of process control in the dyehouse can correct. The greige tube-dye test in section 5 costs perhaps twenty minutes and prevents a full batch re-dye, which at your volume is the single highest-return test in this document.

On the existing apps. The re-dye path already has SAP support in this system: Cancel Production Confirmation reverses the dyeing confirmation and WIP Batch Close reopens the batch. Section 9 wires the quality rejection decision into that existing loop rather than inventing a parallel one.

4. The throughput problem, and tiered testing

This is the constraint that shapes everything else, so it belongs before the characteristic tables rather than after them.

Seventy-five batches a day. The full post-dyeing battery in section 6 is twenty-two characteristics. Run in full on every batch that is 1,650 tests per day, several of which take longer than a day to produce a result — ISO 105-B02 light fastness alone runs for tens of hours of xenon exposure. Any specification that implies testing everything on everything will be quietly abandoned within a fortnight, and you will be left with no quality record at all.

So the battery is tiered. Three tiers, applied per characteristic:

TierFrequencyRationaleDaily volume at 75 batches
TIER 1
In-line		Every batch, 100%Fast, cheap, catches the failures that actually occur day to day. Result available within the batch cycle.~7 characteristics × 75 = 525 results
TIER 2
Shade qualification		First batch of each shade / recipe, and on any recipe changeFastness is a property of the dye recipe, not of the individual batch. Testing it once per recipe is scientifically correct and commercially sufficient.~8–12 new shades/day × 10 = ~100 results
TIER 3
Periodic & compliance		Weekly, monthly, per buyer lot, or per certification cycleRegulatory and long-duration tests. Formaldehyde, azo amines, light fastness. Often sent to an external accredited laboratory.Handful per week
The resulting load is manageable. Roughly 625 result entries per day. On a tablet designed for the job — batch chosen by scan, one tap per grey-scale rating, numeric pad for the rest — that is about 4–5 seconds per result, or under an hour of total data entry across the day. That is the design target for the apps in section 11, and it is the number to hold me to.
Tier 2 depends on a reliable shade/recipe identifier. The apps must know whether the batch in front of the technician is the first of its recipe today. ZPP_BATCHN carries DYE_CODE but I have not yet confirmed whether the dye recipe is separately identified — the Recipe Master app suggests it is. This needs settling before tier 2 can be automated; until then it falls to the technician to flag, which is less reliable.

Why fastness is tier 2 and not tier 1. Colour fastness is a property of the dye chemistry and the wash-off process, not of the individual batch. Two batches of the same recipe run correctly will not differ in fastness. Testing it once per recipe — and re-testing whenever the recipe, dye lot or supplier changes — is both scientifically sound and defensible in a buyer audit, provided the linkage from batch to qualified recipe is recorded. That linkage is what the app must guarantee.

Position matters more than count. In package dyeing the dominant failure mode is a gradient — inside to outside, and top to bottom of the carrier. Three samples chosen to span that gradient tell you far more than ten chosen at random. The apps should therefore prompt for position, not just for a package number, and record it against the result.

9.2 The re-dye loop, using apps you already have

An RD or ST decision at stage 2 must reopen the batch for correction. That path exists in this system already and should not be rebuilt:

  1. Post-Dyeing QC app records the usage decision RD and blocks the batch.
  2. The batch must be reopened before the confirmation can be reversed — this is the dependency the plant runbook records. WIP Batch Close app, Reopen action, reason code QC-REDYE.
  3. Cancel Production Confirmation reverses the dyeing confirmation, writing the reversal to AFRU.
  4. Correction is dyed; a new inspection lot is raised automatically on re-confirmation.
  5. Re-dyed batches carry a mandatory tenacity re-check — each thermal cycle costs strength, and two corrections on the same batch is normally the point to stop.
Rule worth adopting: two corrections maximum. After a second re-dye, require a QC Manager signature to attempt a third. Polyester tolerates repeated HT cycles better than most fibres, but tenacity and hand deteriorate, and the economics have usually gone negative well before the quality does.

Why a draft table. A fastness battery is not entered in one sitting — a technician starts a lot, the perspirometer runs for four hours, they come back. QM has no draft concept for results: a result is either recorded or it is not. Without it the technician must either hold results on paper or record them prematurely, and both defeat the purpose.

Design decisions. The plant batch number leads, because that is what is written on the carrier tag — the QM inspection lot number is secondary and shown only in the detail. Shade name and machine are on the row because technicians triage by them. The first of recipe flag is calculated server-side and drives which characteristics appear, so tier 2 is enforced by the system rather than remembered by a person.

Design decisions. The standard and the spec limit sit on every row, so the technician never has to remember a limit or consult a folder. Valuation is immediate and colour-coded — a fail is visible the moment it is entered, not after posting. Grey-scale ratings are a nine-position tap strip, never a dropdown; that is the difference between one second and five per result, and across 625 results a day it is the difference between the app being used and being abandoned. A failing characteristic demands a defect code before the lot can be posted, which is what makes the Pareto analysis in 11.4 possible.

11.3 Screen 3 — Usage decision

Summary of all characteristics with their valuations, the proposed decision derived from the rules in section 9, and the technician's confirmation or override. An override requires a reason. RD and ST decisions display the re-dye loop from 9.2 as an explicit next-step prompt, including the reason code to use in WIP Batch Close.
